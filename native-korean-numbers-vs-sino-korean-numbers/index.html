Native Korean Numbers vs Sino-Korean Numbers, and When to Use Which

How Korean's two parallel number systems actually split by use case and by size, based on guidance from Korea's National Institute of Korean Language.

Learn Korean DailyOct 9, 2026 · 4 min read

Korean is one of relatively few languages with two complete, actively used number systems running in parallel: a native Korean set (하나, 둘, 셋...) and a Sino-Korean set borrowed from Chinese numerals (일, 이, 삼...). Learners usually get told "use native numbers for counting things, Sino-Korean for everything else," which is a reasonable starting rule but not the whole picture. Korea's National Institute of Korean Language (국립국어원), the government body that sets standard guidance on the Korean language, addresses the finer points directly in its public language Q&A service.

The baseline split that gets taught first

The commonly taught division, and a reasonable starting point, is:

  • Native Korean numbers (하나, 둘, 셋, 넷, 다섯...) are used for counting objects, people, and age, typically paired with a counter word (개, 명, 살, and others).
  • Sino-Korean numbers (일, 이, 삼, 사, 오...) are used for dates, money amounts, phone numbers, addresses, and most numbers of 100 or more.

This covers a large share of everyday usage correctly: counting apples uses 하나, 둘, 셋; telling someone a price uses 일, 이, 삼.

Where it gets genuinely contested, even among native speakers

The National Institute of Korean Language's own public Q&A service shows this split isn't as rigid as the simplified version suggests, because the Institute itself fields repeated questions from Korean speakers about exactly where the native-to-Sino-Korean switch happens at larger numbers.

One question submitted to the Institute asked specifically how to read "214명" (214 people) out loud, noting that an AI tool had given two different answers on two different days: "이백십네 명" using a mixed native-Sino-Korean construction, versus "이백열네 명" or "이백십사 명." The Institute's official answer, through its 온라인가나다 (Online Ganada) language consultation service, is worth reading closely because it does not give a single rigid rule. It states plainly that there is no formal written language-standard (어문 규범) that fixes exactly how to read numbers aloud in this kind of mixed case, so the Institute's answer leans on documented real-world usage patterns instead of declaring one form "correct" and the rest "wrong."

What the Institute's real-usage guidance says

According to that official answer, "214명" is generally read as "이백십사 명," using the Sino-Korean number construction throughout. At the same time, the Institute notes that because 명 (the counter for people) can also take a native Korean number form, you will also hear "이백열네 명," which mixes the Sino-Korean hundred-and-ten (이백십) with the native-style units digit (열네, "fourteen" in the native style used before certain counters).

The Institute's broader guidance, stated directly in its answer, is the most useful practical takeaway: as numbers get smaller, native Korean number forms are used more naturally; as numbers get larger, Sino-Korean number forms become the natural choice. There isn't a hard cutoff point written into the language standards; it's a gradient of natural usage, not a strict rule with a fixed boundary number.

Why this matters for a learner, practically

For a learner, the Institute's own acknowledgment that even native speakers disagree on the exact crossover point in numbers like 214 is actually reassuring. It means getting a mixed or "wrong" sounding number construction in casual conversation around the hundreds range is not likely to cause real confusion, since native speakers themselves use more than one accepted form in that exact range.

The clearer, lower-stakes rule to rely on day to day is this: for small numbers tied to a counter (age, number of people at a table, number of items you want to order), default to native Korean numbers. For money, phone numbers, dates, addresses, and any number that's clearly in the hundreds or above, default to Sino-Korean numbers. In the genuinely ambiguous middle ground, which the Institute's own guidance confirms exists, both forms are used by native speakers, and listening for which one a given conversation partner uses is a reasonable way to pick up the local convention rather than trying to memorize a hard boundary that the language standards themselves don't actually specify.

A short reference for the most common learner situations

  • Ordering food by quantity ("two of these"): native Korean number plus counter, for example 두 개.
  • Giving your age: native Korean number, for example 스물다섯 살 (25 years old).
  • Giving a phone number or a price: Sino-Korean numbers throughout, digit by digit for phone numbers.
  • Telling the time (the hour): native Korean numbers, for example 한 시 (1 o'clock); minutes use Sino-Korean numbers, for example 삼십 분 (30 minutes).
  • Counting people in the hundreds, per the Institute's own answer: Sino-Korean is the more common form, though a mixed native-style form for the final digit is also heard.

Key takeaways

  • Korean uses two full, actively used number systems: native Korean for counting with everyday counters and age, Sino-Korean for money, dates, phone numbers, and large numbers.
  • The National Institute of Korean Language's own public guidance confirms there is no fixed written-language standard dictating exactly how to read every number aloud, especially in the hundreds range.
  • For a number like 214 paired with the counter 명, native speakers use both "이백십사 명" (fully Sino-Korean) and "이백열네 명" (mixed), according to the Institute's own answer.
  • The Institute's practical framing is a gradient, not a hard cutoff: smaller numbers lean native, larger numbers lean Sino-Korean, with real ambiguity in between.
  • Telling time splits the two systems within a single phrase: native Korean for the hour, Sino-Korean for the minutes.

Treat the native-versus-Sino-Korean split as a strong tendency rather than an exact rule, especially once numbers climb into the hundreds, since that's exactly where Korea's own language authority says the usage genuinely varies.
